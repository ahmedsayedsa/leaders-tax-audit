How to Verify a VAT Registration in Egypt Before You Pay

November 2026 · 8 min read
Quick Answer

Verifying a VAT registration means confirming that the supplier you buy from is genuinely registered with the Egyptian Tax Authority, because your input VAT deduction is only valid against invoices from a registered supplier. Verify by enquiring with the tax registration number — nine digits — through the Tax Authority's online portal, by observing the supplier's status inside the e-invoicing system, and by matching their invoice data against their official documents and registration certificate.

Every purchase invoice carrying VAT is, for you, a promise of a deduction: you paid the tax to the supplier today, and you will deduct it from your sales tax tomorrow. But that promise has one non-negotiable condition — the supplier must actually be registered for VAT. If you buy from a party who prints a number on their invoices without being registered, or whose registration was cancelled, you discover at audit that your deduction is rejected and that the tax you thought you had recovered has become a cost on you, retroactively. Verifying a VAT registration is not extra bureaucracy; it is the safety valve protecting the most valuable right the tax system grants you: input tax deduction.

And the paradox is that verification costs almost nothing compared with what it protects. A few minutes of enquiry and matching before you approve a new supplier, plus a simple periodic routine for existing ones, spare your company audit differences discovered years later, when there is no longer any way to go back to the supplier for anything. This article explains the practical verification routes, the data you must match, the warning signs, and how to act if you discover the problem late. Why must you verify a supplier's registration before dealing with them?

The VAT system is built as a chain: every link deducts what it paid to the previous link. That chain only works if every link is genuinely inside the system. An unregistered supplier has no legal right to charge you tax in the first place, and the invoice they issue — however professional it looks — creates no deduction right for you. At audit, the tax office examines your purchase invoices and checks their issuers; if the issuer is not registered, the input tax on those invoices is rejected, the cost falls on you, in addition to whatever late-payment charges and penalties the governing rules attach to the differences.

To grasp the exposure in real figures: a single purchase invoice of EGP 100,000 carries EGP 14,000 of VAT at the standard 14% rate. If that supplier is part of your regular monthly purchases and you keep deducting their tax for a full year, the deduction at risk of rejection alone reaches EGP 168,000 — before any late-payment charges or penalties, and before asking what that does to financial statements presented to banks. Set that figure against the cost of verification: one enquiry and a document match when the supplier is approved. There is no clearer equation in financial management in favour of prevention.

Nor does the loss stop at the rejected tax. Discovering one unregistered supplier in a single audited year opens the door to reviewing every year of dealing with them, and puts your entire purchasing function under the microscope. That is why we treat registration verification as part of approving the supplier itself, not a step after it: just as you check a supplier's ability to deliver and their quality, you check their tax soundness — because the effect of the second shows up in your returns, not in their warehouse. The same philosophy governs our tax and tax audit service: prevention at the document-approval stage is always cheaper than defence at audit.

How do you verify registration through the Tax Authority's portal?

The first official channel is the enquiry services available on the Egyptian Tax Authority's online portal, eta.gov.eg, where you can enquire by tax registration number about a registrant's data and status. The sound working method:

  1. Request the registration number from the supplier directly as part of their approval documents, together with a copy of the registration certificate — a genuinely registered supplier provides both without hesitation, because they are part of their commercial identity.
  2. Enter the number into the enquiry service on the portal exactly as written on the certificate, and compare the result: is the number tied to a name matching the supplier's trade name, and is the registration status active?
  3. Document the verification result in the supplier's file with its date; at any future audit you will have proof that you exercised due care at the time of dealing.
  4. Repeat the verification periodically for key suppliers, because a registration's status can change over time, and a one-off check at the start of the relationship does not cover later years.

Note that enquiring with a wrong or incomplete number yields a misleading result; if the number returns no data, the first possibility is that the number itself is incorrect — which is itself information that obliges you to ask the supplier before completing any purchase. Do not rely on an old invoice you lifted the number from; take it from the certificate itself.

How does the e-invoicing system help you verify?

Since the e-invoicing system went live, dealings between registered companies run through the Tax Authority's platform, invoicing.eta.gov.eg, which validates invoices in real time as they are issued and received. This system gives you an additional verification layer that needs no separate effort from you: a supplier who issues your invoices through the system appears in it as a registered party with the data recorded for them at the Authority, and the invoice that reaches you through it carries data attested by the system itself, not by a template the supplier designed.

Two channels are often confused here and must be kept apart: the e-invoice governs dealings between companies, while the e-receipt governs dealings with the final consumer. If your supplier sells to you in your capacity as a company, the correct route for their invoices is the e-invoicing system; if you buy from an outlet that deals with the public by nature, its receipts may follow the other route. What you must not accept is a document belonging to neither system from a party who should be inside one of them: self-designed documents outside any official platform are the weakest link in your purchase file at audit, however complete their formal data looks.

The other side matters just as much: if the supplier's activity falls within the system's mandated phases yet they insist on paper invoices or files outside the system, that behaviour itself is a question mark.

There may be a legitimate explanation — an activity not yet in scope, or consumer-facing dealings governed by the e-receipt rather than the e-invoice — but it is an explanation you should hear and verify, not skip. A Rule We Work By

No deduction before verification. Adopt a simple rule in your accounting system: the tax on a purchase invoice is not posted to a deductible input VAT account before the supplier's verification file is complete — registration certificate, enquiry result, and a matched first invoice. Anything posted earlier stays suspended until the file is complete.

Which data must you match on the supplier's invoice?

Digital verification through the portal and the system is half the task; the other half is traditional document matching that no disciplined finance function can do without. When you receive a new supplier's invoices, match three elements against their official documents:

  • The trade name: it must match the name recorded on the registration certificate and the supplier's commercial register; a material difference in wording may mean you are dealing with a different entity from the one you verified.
  • The tax registration number: nine complete digits, constant across all invoices, matching the certificate's number exactly — one different digit means a different entity in the system's eyes.
  • The address and premises: matching the official documents, as they form part of the registrant's identity recorded at the Authority.

Keep in each supplier's file one tidy bundle: the commercial register, the registration certificate, the dated enquiry result, and a sample first matched invoice. This bundle is your first line of defence at any audit of your purchases; when the tax office finds a file documented this way it moves on to other items, and when it finds invoices with no documented verification at all, it widens the audit. Organizing these files is an integral part of sound bookkeeping and accounting, not an extra task beside it.

Which warning signs should make you stop?

From audit files in practice, certain signs recur in cases of unregistered suppliers or those whose status is ambiguous. Any one of them does not mean instant condemnation, but it does mean pausing dealings until the position is clear:

  • An invoice with no tax registration number that nonetheless charges you VAT explicitly — whoever has no number has no right to charge the tax.
  • A registration number short of digits, or one that changes between invoices without documentary explanation.
  • Refusal or stalling over sharing the registration certificate when requested among the approval documents; it is a document a registrant is proud of, not one they hide.
  • Tax charged at unusual rates or formats, or in convoluted separate lines inside the invoice.
  • Insistence on dealing outside the e-invoicing system despite their activity being mandated into it, or on full cash settlement with no documentary trail.

Our professional view: a single sign of these deserves a pause, and two together mean ending dealings until further notice. No deal's margin, however tempting, compensates for a rejected deduction across years of purchases.

What should you do if you discover your supplier is not registered?

Discovering the problem late is better than the tax office discovering it. If you verify and find that a supplier you have dealt with is not registered, the correct order of action is:

  1. Stop deducting immediately: do not deduct tax on any new invoices from this supplier, and review what was actually deducted in earlier returns to size the exposure precisely.
  2. Document everything: gather the invoices, the course of dealing, and whatever documents the supplier showed you at the time; documented good faith changes the nature of the treatment at audit.
  3. Write to the supplier formally to correct their position or prove their registration if your enquiry was mistaken, and keep the correspondence.
  4. Correct the affected returns with sound technical treatment before an audit reaches them; taking the initiative to correct is a far stronger position than waiting.
  5. Review your entire supplier list by the same method — a single unregistered supplier is rarely the only one when no verification procedure existed in the first place.

Where the problem intersects with an ongoing audit or open years, managing the file becomes a matter of technical representation before the tax office — the core of our work in audit and assurance and tax. The conclusion: input deduction is an earned right for those who verify, and a deferred risk for those who assume. Make registration verification the gateway to approving every supplier, and your purchase invoices turn from a potential weakness into a documented file that works in your favour.
