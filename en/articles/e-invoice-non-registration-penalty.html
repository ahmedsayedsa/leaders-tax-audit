Penalty for Not Registering in Egypt's E-Invoice System

November 2026 · 8 min read
Quick Answer

The fine for not registering in Egypt's e-invoice system — or for not issuing invoices electronically — is EGP 20,000 to EGP 100,000, under Article 71 of the Unified Tax Procedures Law No. 206 of 2020. More serious than the fine itself: paper invoices have not been accepted as proof of costs and expenses since 1 April 2023. And there is no daily fine anywhere in the law — that widely shared figure is simply wrong.

Many business owners ask us the same question in different words: "I still haven't registered for e-invoicing — what can actually happen to me?" The honest answer is that the real danger is not the fine alone. The fine is a known amount with a statutory ceiling, but the bigger harm reaches your company quietly: invoices you cannot prove as expenses, input VAT that becomes difficult to deduct, and large clients who stop dealing with you because their systems accept nothing but an electronic invoice. This guide sets out the penalty from the text of the law first, then the practical damage most late registrants never see coming, then exactly what to do if you were obliged to register and have not.

Before the penalty, keep in mind that the system's mandate connects to the tax registration thresholds themselves. If the "EGP 250K or 500K?" question is on your mind, our guide on the e-invoice registration threshold corrects it in full — confusion between those two figures is one of the most common causes of delay we see in practice.

What does the law actually require before we talk about penalties?

The penalty does not come out of nowhere; it attaches to two clear duties in the Unified Tax Procedures Law No. 206 of 2020. The first duty is in Article 35, which obliges designated companies, legal persons, and natural persons to record their sales and purchases on the electronic system specified by the Egyptian Tax Authority. In other words, once your business falls within an obliged category, recording your transactions on the system is a legal duty, not an organisational choice.

The second duty is in Article 37, which obliges every taxpayer to issue tax invoices or receipts in electronic form. Note that the text is not only about "registering" — it governs the form of the document itself: the invoice you issue to your client must be issued electronically through the system if you are among the obliged. A company that registered in the system but kept issuing paper invoices to its clients has not fully complied.

Our professional view here is clear: many companies treated the system as a formality that could wait "until things settle", and that postponement is exactly what turns a simple duty into a documented violation. Registration is a procedural step with limited cost and time; climbing out of a violation after it happens is far harder.

How much is the fine for not registering in the e-invoice system?

Article 71 of the Unified Tax Procedures Law sets a fine of no less than EGP 20,000 and no more than EGP 100,000 for violating Articles 35 and 37. The Egyptian Tax Authority itself publishes this range in its own awareness materials, so it is neither an estimate by accounting firms nor a sourceless rumour. The table below summarises the penalty and its direct legal consequences:

Violation or consequenceLegal basisResult
Failure to record sales and purchases on the electronic systemArticle 35, Law 206 of 2020Fine of EGP 20,000–100,000 (Article 71)
Failure to issue the tax invoice or receipt in electronic formArticle 37, Law 206 of 2020Fine of EGP 20,000–100,000 (Article 71)

Notice that the fine's range is deliberately wide: from twenty thousand to one hundred thousand pounds. That means the final amount is not the same for every violator, and the size of your activity, the nature of the violation, and its duration all enter the practical picture. For exactly that reason we never advise a business owner to "price it in" at the minimum; the exposure is not EGP 20,000 as many assume — it can reach five times that, on top of the other consequences detailed below.

Is there a daily fine for every day of delay?

No. We stress this because many social-media posts cite daily amounts that supposedly accumulate with every day of delay, and there is no provision in the Unified Tax Procedures Law for a daily fine on e-invoice registration violations. The penalty in Article 71 is a sum falling between a minimum and a maximum — not a daily meter.

But do not let correcting that claim make you complacent. The fine does not accumulate daily, yet the damage accumulates in another way: every month you spend outside the system is another month of invoices you will not be able to prove, purchases whose paid VAT is at risk in deduction, and sales to clients who require electronic invoices that you may lose to a registered competitor. The fine has a fixed ceiling; the operational loss is open-ended.

Important Warning

If any content reaches you threatening a specific daily fine for e-invoice registration, treat it with caution: it either confuses different systems and laws, or uses fear as a marketing tool. Always ask for the article number and the source of the text before believing any figure.

What happened to your paper invoices after 1 April 2023?

Since 1 April 2023, paper invoices are no longer accepted as proof of costs and expenses when filing the income tax return. The practical meaning of that sentence is larger than it looks: costs and expenses are what reduce your taxable base, so if you cannot prove them with accepted documents, you pay tax on a profit larger than your real profit. In plain terms, your company may genuinely have earned a thin margin, yet be taxed as though its costs were lower than they were.

This consequence does not need a violation to be "discovered" before it bites; it happens automatically through the way costs are proven. Many companies discovered it late — while preparing the income tax return or at their first audit — when they found that entire months of purchases and expenses were documented on paper only. That is why we consider this consequence more dangerous than the fine itself: the fine is capped at EGP 100,000, while additional tax on disallowed costs can exceed that depending on the size of your activity.

What practical damage never appears in the fine itself?

Even if the fine were never imposed on you, staying outside the system costs your company on four practical fronts we repeatedly see with late clients:

  • Input VAT deduction: Your right to deduct the VAT you paid on purchases depends on valid documents. Dealing outside the system makes that VAT harder to prove, and can cost you amounts you actually paid simply because their documents do not meet the required form. To confirm your registration position in the first place, see the steps for verifying VAT registration.
  • Dealing with large companies and government bodies: Major companies and government entities require electronic invoices in their dealings, because they themselves can only prove their purchases with them. An unregistered supplier is quietly dropped from vendor lists — sometimes without ever learning why deals slipped away.
  • Risk classification at audit: A violator of the system's obligations is classified among the high-risk cases in tax audits, which means deeper scrutiny, wider audit years, and more questions. An audit is not a disaster when your papers are in order, but it becomes costly and draining when you start from the position of the violator.
  • Banking and finance relationships: Financial statements backed by electronically documented invoices are easier to rely on when seeking finance or facilities; an electronic document verified in real time by the Tax Authority is stronger evidence than a paper document nobody can verify.

Taken together, this damage is why we always tell our clients: do not compare the cost of registration with the fine — compare it with the cost of staying outside the system for a full year. The second comparison tolerates no delay.

Who is obliged to register in the system?

The e-invoice mandate was not issued for all companies at once; it came in successive phases, through decisions specifying the obliged categories in each phase. There is therefore no single answer that fits every activity and size: your company may have entered the mandate in an early or a late phase depending on its activity and nature, and some categories were tied to specific dates in the decisions issued for them.

A common mistake happens here: relying on a rule the business owner heard from a post, or from a peer in a different activity. Your position is determined by the phase decisions and the Tax Authority's published lists — not by impressions. If your activity falls within a category covered by a mandate decision, you are obliged from that decision's effective date, even if no personal notice reached you. Not knowing your mandate date does not suspend the obligation.

For those not yet covered by any decision, early voluntary registration remains, in our view, a smart step: it gives your team time to train on issuing without deadline pressure, and spares you the congestion that typically happens as new phase deadlines approach.

How do you avoid the penalty if you are already late?

If you discover your company was obliged and has not registered, the right move is immediate, organised action — not waiting for the audit to arrive. The practical steps we carry out with clients in our e-invoice and e-receipt service are:

  1. Confirm the mandate position: Review the phase decisions and the Authority's lists to pin down your exact mandate date, because arranging earlier periods starts from there.
  2. Register in the system: Create your company's account on the e-invoice environment and complete its core data.
  3. Obtain the electronic signature: No approved invoices can be issued without a valid electronic signature or seal in the company's name.
  4. Code your items and services: Map your products and services to the approved classification codes (GS1 or EGS) so your invoices are accepted without errors.
  5. Connect your accounting system: Link your accounting software or ERP to the system if your invoice volume is large, or start issuing directly through the portal for small volumes.
  6. Review earlier periods with your accountant: Assess how the months you operated outside the system affect your returns and deduction position, and put a documented remediation plan in place.

If you are not registered for VAT at all despite having crossed the registration threshold, start there first; the steps are laid out in our guide on how to register for VAT. The correct ordering of the whole tax file — registration, then the system, then regular issuing — is what actually protects you, not any single step on its own.

Our bottom line: the financial penalty is known and capped, but every day of delay adds operational and tax damage that never appears in the text of Article 71. Registering today is a simple procedure; registering after an audit is a defensive position. Choose to be in the first position.
